An independent research hub

Two tracks ran through Islamic civilization. Most histories only follow one.

This project reads Islamic history through an institutional lens rather than a doctrinal one: alongside the formal record of dynasties, armies, and courts, a parallel infrastructure of hereditary religious prestige, endowment economies, and scholarly transmission chains shaped how authority actually moved. Reading it this way is a question about institutions, not a claim about whose theology was correct.

Read the framework Publications & ORCID

"Track 2 is not a hidden truth beneath Sunni or Shiʿi Islam. It is a way of asking who actually held authority, and how it was transmitted, that the Sunni/Shiʿi frame alone doesn't answer."

Sectarian historiography asks which doctrine a given ruler, order, or scholar held. This project asks a different, adjacent question: which of two coexisting systems of authority — the dynastic-legal state, or the genealogical and charismatic network running alongside it — actually explains a given institution's survival, funding, and reach. The two questions can have different answers for the same historical figure, and neither one is secretly the other.

The framework

01 — reading Islamic institutional history in two tracks
Track 1

The formal state

Dynastic and imperial authority: caliphates, sultanates, and their successor states, legitimated by conquest, treaty, and codified law.

  • Court-appointed judiciary and state madhhab
  • Kharāj / jizya fiscal administration
  • Standing armies and provincial governors
  • Dynastic succession and imperial chancery
Track 2

The genealogical network

Authority carried by descent, endowment, and transmission rather than office: a parallel infrastructure that outlasted most of the dynasties built on Track 1.

  • Sayyid / Sadat lineage and the naqīb al-ashrāf registries
  • Waqf endowments funding shrines, canals, and madrasas
  • Sufi order silsilas and isnād-based scholarly transmission
  • Shrine (dargāh) networks as regional infrastructure
What this framework is not claiming: that any specific ruler, order, or school was secretly of one sect or another absent direct evidence, or that Track 2 constitutes a literal shadow government. It is an analytic distinction for institutional history, applied case by case — not a master key that resolves every question in advance.

A transmission chain, followed across the line

02 — sanad, n. a chain of authorities behind a transmitted text

One real, checkable case: Ṭūsī's kalām primer picked up a Twelver commentator (al-Ḥillī) and a Sunni Ashʿarī one (al-Jurjānī) in the same transmission history, and the resulting text was taught across sectarian lines for centuries. This is the kind of institutional fact the framework is built to notice — not asserted, traced.

Codicology & terms

06 — defined precisely, not rhetorically
Sanad

The chain of named authorities behind a transmitted text or report — the basic unit of verification in the manuscript tradition this scriptorium studies, and the source of this hub's working method.

Walāya

Authority or guardianship transmitted through recognized descent or spiritual filiation, distinct from office-based authority.

Sadat

Recognized descendants of the Prophet through Fāṭima and ʿAlī — a real, historically documented genealogical and social category.

Naqīb al-Ashrāf

The historical office responsible for registering and governing sayyid lineages under Ottoman, Safavid, and Mughal administration.
