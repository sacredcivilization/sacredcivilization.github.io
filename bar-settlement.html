Tier III — Essay

Who Actually Settled the Bār? Kings, Canals, and a Six-Century Chronology Problem

Saad Khizar Bosal Alvid Scriptorium verification pass complete
The argument, stated plainly A recurring claim in this project's source material holds that the vast interior scrubland of the Punjab Doābs — the "Bār," some 80 percent of the region's landmass — sat outside every pre-modern state's reach until 13th-century Sādāt shrine-masters sedentarized its pastoral clans through canal-building, unconditional feeding at the Langar, and spiritual discipleship. The underlying geography and the fiscal categories involved are real. But the claim's actual chronology does not survive a check against the Bār region's own documented settlement history, which places its large-scale conversion from pastoral wilderness to farmland squarely in the British colonial canal-colony era — roughly six hundred years after this project's earlier draft placed it.

1. What is solid: the kings, the geography, the legal categories

The dynasties that ruled the Indus corridor before the Sādāt shrine networks reached their later prominence are real and well documented: the Rai dynasty of Sindh (a little earlier than sometimes stated — closer to 489–632 CE than 524–632), the Brahmin Chach dynasty (632–712 CE, capital at Aror), the Hindu Shahis of Kabul and Punjab (822–1026 CE, moving capitals from Kabul to Udabhanda/Hund to Nandana to Lahore as Ghaznavid pressure mounted), and then the Ghaznavid, Ghurid, and Delhi Sultanate garrisons that held Lahore and Multan. These rulers did concentrate their administration in fortified river cities, and the five interior tracts this project draws on — Sandal Bār, Kirāna Bār, Ganjī Bār, Nīlī Bār, and Gondal Bār — are real, independently documented regions of thorny scrubland between the rivers, historically the range of semi-nomadic pastoral clans.

The fiscal vocabulary is also sound. Waqf's classical definition — the corpus of an asset detained in perpetuity while its usufruct is freed for a stated purpose — is standard fiqh, and its inalienability is real. Khums as a twenty percent Twelver Shiʿi levy, split between the Sahm al-Imām and Sahm al-Sādāt, is likewise standard, well-documented jurisprudence. Both categories are real. What is not documented is the specific mechanism an earlier draft built on top of them: that Sādāt jurists systematically channeled collected Khums into land purchase and canal excavation at a scale that converted private capital into a shrine-controlled agrarian empire. Waqf and Khums existing as categories does not establish that particular institutional pipeline, and no independent source for it turned up here.

2. The claim that doesn't hold: who settled the Bār, and when

The strongest version of the earlier argument credited Bābā Farīd (Farīduddīn Ganjshakar), the 13th-century Chishti master of Ajodhan (present-day Pakpattan), with personally excavating canals into the Ganjī Bār and converting its pastoral clans into settled peasants — citing Amīr Khwurd's 14th-century hagiography Siyar al-Awliyāʾ as the source. Baba Farid's own well-documented legacy is substantial and doesn't need this addition: he is a founding figure of the Chishti order in India, the first major poet to write in Punjabi, and — this part is genuinely well attested — the first to introduce the institution of the Langar to the Punjab region. Nothing in his standard biography, however, documents canal engineering or organized pastoralist resettlement as part of his activity.

More importantly, the region's own settlement history contradicts the larger chronology. Sandal Bār — one of the five tracts named in the earlier draft — was, in the words of its own documented history, "sparsely populated" until the late 19th century, and was only brought under irrigation and settlement "after the establishment of Chenab Colony (Lyallpur) in 1892." This is not an isolated detail: the transformation of Punjab's Bār tracts from pastoral scrubland into some of the subcontinent's most productive farmland is one of the best-documented episodes of British colonial engineering, carried out through the canal-colony system from the 1880s through the 1940s. That process, not a 13th-century Sufi-Sayyid campaign, is what actually converted the interior Bār at scale. Smaller, older inundation canals along the immediate river margins are unremarkable and plausible; an 80-percent-of-landmass sedentarization credited to 13th-century Sādāt canal-building simply predates, by roughly six centuries, the documented moment when that land was actually settled.

3. What I take from this

None of this erases the real thing worth noticing: pre-modern Indus states did concentrate their power in river-city corridors, and the Bār's interior genuinely sat outside that reach for a very long time. Sufi shrines, including Baba Farid's, genuinely did offer food, asylum, and a form of belonging that state power did not extend into pastoral country, and the Langar is a real and significant part of that. But the specific causal story — Sādāt canals sedentarizing the Bār in the 13th century — is not what the documented history of the Bār actually shows. The region's own settlement record points somewhere else: six centuries later, and to a different set of engineers entirely.

Before this material is drawn on elsewhere in the project, any claim about when and by whom the Bār was settled should be checked against the canal-colony historiography (Imran Ali and others) rather than restated from this file.
