Tier II — Framework

Reading the 1930 Allahabad Address: What Iqbal Actually Proposed, and a Track 2 Reading of Why the Indus

Saad Khizar Bosal Alvid Scriptorium verification pass complete

A note on what this is, before anything else: what follows is this project's own interpretive reading, not a forensic finding and not a claim to have "proven" anything about Muhammad Iqbal's private intentions. The first half of this essay is a plain historical account of what Iqbal actually said in December 1930, checked against the primary text and mainstream Iqbal scholarship — that part is reported as fact because it is verifiable fact. The second half asks whether the specific geography Iqbal chose is worth reading, in addition to its obvious demographic and federalist logic, against the older institutional history of Sayyid shrines and walāya-based authority in the Indus corridor. That second part is argument, not record. I have not found a single instance of Iqbal himself invoking dargāhs, Sayyid lineages, or Alid walāya as his reason for the North-West demarcation, and I say so plainly rather than implying otherwise. Readers should weigh the second half as a proposed lens, not treat it as what Iqbal secretly meant.

The argument, stated plainly

On 29 December 1930, Muhammad Iqbal, as president of the All-India Muslim League, proposed consolidating Punjab, the North-West Frontier Province, Sindh, and Baluchistan into a single autonomous Muslim-majority unit within a still-federated British India. That proposal is real, its text survives, and its ordinary political logic — Muslim demographic majority, linguistic and economic coherence, defensibility as a self-governing federal unit — is well documented and does not need embellishing. It is also, as a matter of plain chronology, a different act from Chaudhry Rahmat Ali's 1933 coinage of the word "Pakistan" and from the Muslim League's 1940 Lahore Resolution calling for separate, sovereign states — three distinct moments that popular memory frequently compresses into one, and that this essay keeps apart on purpose.

Underneath that record, I want to make a narrower, clearly-labeled argument: that the specific quadrant of territory Iqbal named — the Indus basin, running from the Punjab's five rivers down through Sindh to the Arabian Sea, edged by Baluchistan and the Frontier — was also, independently of Iqbal's own stated reasoning, a region with a genuinely old and well-documented history of Sayyid-lineage shrine authority (Multan, Uch Sharīf, Pakpattan, Sehwan) running in parallel to whatever dynasty formally ruled it. That older institutional pattern is real and separately verifiable. Whether Iqbal was consciously or unconsciously drawing on it — whether his geography and that older Sayyid-shrine geography overlapping is meaningful or coincidental — is a question I can raise and argue is worth taking seriously, but not one I can answer with anything Iqbal himself said. That is the honest shape of what follows.

1. What Iqbal actually said, and to whom

Iqbal delivered the address as outgoing president of the All-India Muslim League at its annual session in Allahabad. The core proposal, in his own words, was:

"I would like to see the Punjab, North-West Frontier Province, Sind and Baluchistan amalgamated into a single State. Self-government within the British Empire, or without the British Empire, the formation of a consolidated North-West Indian Muslim State appears to me to be the final destiny of the Muslims, at least of North-West India."

This is a real, independently locatable quotation — the full text of the address is publicly available and widely reprinted. Iqbal was speaking into the specific context of the Round Table Conference process then underway in London, arguing against both a unitary, numerically-majoritarian Indian nationalism (which he thought would leave Muslims permanently outvoted) and against the Simon Commission's proposed communal-electorate arrangements, which he considered inadequate protection for Muslim-majority provinces like Punjab and Bengal. His proposal was explicitly for autonomy within a federated British India — a reorganization of provincial boundaries to secure a durable Muslim-majority unit — not a call for outright partition or an independent sovereign state. That distinction is the mainstream historiographical consensus and is worth stating precisely, because so much retrospective reading collapses it.

Iqbal's own stated criteria, in the address, were: consolidated demographic majority, "unity of language, race, history, religion and identity of economic interests," and — pointedly — the military and administrative significance of Punjab, whose population supplied a large share of the Indian Army's combat recruits. Bengal, despite having the single largest Muslim population in British India, was not folded into the same territorial proposal; Iqbal nonetheless argued elsewhere in the same address, and in later writing, that Bengal's own Muslim majority also deserved constitutional protection as its own case, on the same federalist logic, not as an inferior or excluded category.

Also on the record and worth flattening a common conflation: Iqbal never used the word "Pakistan" in this speech, because the word did not yet exist. Chaudhry Rahmat Ali, a Cambridge-based Punjabi activist unconnected to the 1930 address, coined it three years later, in January 1933, in a pamphlet titled Now or Never: Are We to Live or Perish Forever?, as an acronym built from Punjab, Afghania (the Frontier), Kashmir, Sindh, and -stan (from Baluchistan) — notably, a scheme that, unlike Iqbal's, explicitly included Kashmir and called for full independent sovereignty from the outset, and that the Muslim League leadership of the time did not immediately embrace. The 1940 Lahore Resolution — the Muslim League's own formal call for sovereign Muslim states — came seven years after that, and ten years after Iqbal's speech. Treating "Iqbal's 1930 Address," "Rahmat Ali's 1933 pamphlet," and "the 1940 Lahore Resolution" as three separable moments in an argument that only later crystallized into Pakistan, rather than as one continuous act of naming, is both the accurate history and, I think, a small useful correction to make explicitly, since conflating them is one of the most common simplifications in popular accounts of the Pakistan Movement.

2. Iqbal's own philosophical case, and where it actually comes from

Iqbal grounded his political argument in a broader philosophical claim, made throughout the address and consistent with his other writing: that Islam is not, in his account, a private religious sentiment separable from social and legal order, and that a nationalism modeled on the post-Westphalian European nation-state ("if it means a displacement of the Islamic principle of solidarity") was, in his words, "simply unthinkable to a Muslim." That is a real and well-attested Iqbal position, expressed across his career — his critique of waṭaniyyat (territorial patriotism) as a kind of idolatry runs through his Urdu and Persian poetry as well as his prose, and is a standard topic in Iqbal scholarship, not a claim particular to this corpus.

Where does that philosophical position come from? Iqbal's 1908 doctoral dissertation at Munich, The Development of Metaphysics in Persia, supervised by the orientalist Fritz Hommel, is a real, published, and still-available work — one of the earlier serious Western-academic engagements with the history of Persian philosophy, including material on Mullā Ṣadrā Shīrāzī (1571–1640) and the Illuminationist and Isfahan traditions. Iqbal's later concept of Khūdī (the dynamic, self-actualizing Ego), developed at length in Asrār-i Khūdī (1915) and elaborated in The Reconstruction of Religious Thought in Islam (1930/1934), does draw on ideas resembling Ṣadrā's doctrine of substantial motion (al-ḥaraka al-jawhariyya) — this connection between Iqbal and Ṣadrian metaphysics is a genuine, mainstream strand of Iqbal scholarship, not a discovery original to this project.

It is important to be precise about what that connection does and doesn't establish. It is good evidence that Iqbal was seriously read in classical Persian philosophy, including a philosopher (Ṣadrā) who is central to later Twelver Shia intellectual tradition. It is not, on its own, evidence about Iqbal's sectarian identity or about any specific political program tied to Sayyid lineages or shrine networks. Iqbal is documented in mainstream biography as coming from a family of Kashmiri Pandit (specifically Sapru-clan) descent that had converted to Islam generations earlier — a heritage he referenced proudly and repeatedly in his own poetry — and as belonging, by the consistent account of standard biographical scholarship, to the Sunni tradition, within its Sufi-inflected Punjabi devotional register. His poetry's frequent, passionate veneration of ʿAlī, Fāṭima, and Ḥusayn — real, well-documented, and genuinely central to his Persian masterworks Rumūz-i Bī-Khūdī and Jāvīdnāma — is not, by itself, a marker of Shia or "Imamological" identity: devotional love for the Ahl al-Bayt, including elaborate poetic treatment of Karbala, is a mainstream and widespread feature of Sufi-inflected Sunni piety across South Asia, present in Barelvi, Chishti, Qadiri, and other Sunni devotional traditions quite independently of Twelver doctrine. Reading that devotional vocabulary as evidence of a covert Shia-coded political program is, I think, a real interpretive overreach, and I want to name it as such rather than repeat it.

3. A Track 2 reading of the geography — clearly labeled as argument

Here is where I want to shift from record to interpretation, and mark that shift explicitly.

The four-province Indus corridor Iqbal named — Punjab, the Frontier, Sindh, Baluchistan — is also, independently of anything Iqbal said, a region with a genuinely old and reasonably well-documented pattern of hereditary Sayyid-lineage shrine authority running through it: the Chishtī shrine of Bābā Farīd at Pakpattan on the Sutlej; the Suhrawardī shrine of Bahāʾ al-Dīn Zakarīyā at Multan, astride the Chenab-Indus confluence; the Bukhārī and Qādirī Sayyid establishment at Uch Sharīf, on the Punjab-Sindh border, historically influential enough to arbitrate across tribal boundaries in the surrounding region; and the shrine of Lāl Shahbāz Qalandar at Sehwan in Sindh's Lower Indus delta. These are real, named, historically documented institutions, and the broader point that Sufi pirs and Sayyid shrine-holders exercised substantial informal social, economic, and mediating authority in rural Punjab and Sindh well into the colonial period is a genuine and well-established theme in South Asian historiography (David Gilmartin's work on Punjab is the standard reference for this general pattern of pir authority, distinct from — and considerably more modest than — the "Two-Track Walāya-Fiqh" architecture argued elsewhere in this corpus, which I address and set aside below).

A plausible reading, then — and I want to be careful to state it exactly this cautiously — is that the same qualities which gave this corridor its 19th- and 20th-century political coherence (settled agrarian society organized partly through hereditary religious authority rather than only through direct state bureaucracy, strong local identity, a history of only partial and negotiated integration into successive imperial centers from Delhi) may also be part of what gave it demographic and cultural coherence as a natural unit for Iqbal's 1930 proposal, over and above the raw population arithmetic. Old patterns of local authority and identity plausibly shape later political geography in ways that are hard to fully disentangle from "linguistic and economic unity," which is itself already part of what Iqbal named as his criterion. Read this way, the Sayyid-shrine history of the Indus corridor is not evidence of a secret Alid political program hidden inside Iqbal's proposal — I found no such evidence and am not asserting it — but it may be one thread, among the ordinary demographic and federalist ones Iqbal actually named, in what made this particular quadrant of territory feel like a coherent "home" to propose consolidating.

I want to be equally clear about what I am not endorsing from elsewhere in this corpus, because it goes considerably further than the paragraph above and is not supported by anything I could independently verify: the claim that a specific, hidden "Two-Track Walāya-Fiqh" system — with a concealed initiatic chain of transmission to the Twelfth Imam, dual-accounting fiscal circuits secretly routing khums to Najaf and Qom via hawala networks, and "double-articulated" manuscripts with esoteric Shia content hidden beneath a Hanafi surface — governed the Indus corridor for eight centuries, and that Iqbal's 1930 proposal was a deliberate act of converting this specific hidden system into public statehood. I looked for independent, non-corpus sourcing for this architecture and did not find any; every citation for it in the source material traces back only to other papers in the same closed corpus, which is not independent verification. The real, checkable history here — Sayyid shrines as centers of local authority, land-grant immunity, and social mediation — is a genuinely interesting and defensible institutional pattern on its own. It does not require, and the evidence does not support, the much more specific and dramatic claim of a secret parallel Shia jurisprudential state apparatus.

4. What this reading is not claiming

To state the boundary plainly: this essay is not claiming that Iqbal privately intended a Sadat/Alid restoration project and concealed it behind ordinary constitutional language. It is not claiming that his devotional poetry about ʿAlī, Fāṭima, and Karbala functioned as an encoded political lexicon legible only to initiates. It is not claiming that a documented, institutionalized "Two-Track" shadow government of dargāhs, hidden manuscripts, and covert khums remittances ran the Indus valley for eight hundred years. None of that is supported by any source independent of this project's own prior output, and I have set all of it aside rather than soften it.

What remains, and what I do think is worth arguing, is more modest: Iqbal's 1930 proposal is accurately understood, first and foremost, on its own explicit terms — a federalist response to a specific colonial constitutional moment, grounded in demographic majority and cultural-linguistic coherence. Layered on top of that accurate record, the specific quadrant of territory he chose also happens to be a region with an old, real, and separately documented history of Sayyid-shrine authority, and it is a reasonable, clearly-labeled interpretive question — not a settled fact — whether that older pattern of local identity and authority is part of what made the geography cohere as Iqbal saw it, alongside the reasons he actually gave.
